4 · The tree and the plane, side by side

Left: the bookkeeping. Drop 1 unit of weight at the top; at each circle it splits, down-left (blue) and down-right (yellow). Right: the real points on the plane. Every circle on the left is the same-colored dot on the right. Drag the control points.

Rows from the bottom: control points, then each blend row, then B(t). Each blend row has one fewer point, because it is built from the neighbouring pairs of the row below. Control-point dot size = its weight in B(t).

5 · What is t? A clock

Think of the red dot as a car on a road and as the clock on the dashboard, 0 to 1. The curve is just the recording of where the car was at each tick. The coloured dots are ticks every 0.1 of the clock. On the right, the same trip is drawn as two ordinary graphs against : one for the x position and one for the height.

The ticks are equally spaced in time but not on the road: the clock is steady, the car is not. The yellow line is the car's velocity arrow: speed = 3 × its length. Drag P1 far from P0 and watch the first ticks spread out.

6 · Why do we even need t?

Regression tries to write the curve as one function : feed in an x, get one y. Pick a shape, then slide the vertical line. Left: how many y-values the real curve has at that x, and what regression answers. Right: the same curve written with .

Takeaway: when a curve doubles back, one x belongs to several points on it, so no function of x can draw it, whatever the degree. Adding gives every point its own label, so "same x" stops being a clash. For a ball bouncing off a wall the path turns back exactly like this.

7 · Knots on a bouncing ball

A ball bounces between two walls, so its position over time is a zigzag with a sharp corner at every bounce. The dots are noisy measurements. Blue: a cubic spline fitted with evenly spaced knots only. Yellow: the same plus knots placed at the bounces. Slide copies of each bounce knot from 1 to 3 and watch the corner appear.

A cubic is smooth to order 3 − m at a knot repeated m times: m = 1 keeps curvature continuous, m = 2 allows a kink in curvature, m = 3 allows a corner in the path itself. The lower plot shows the bump functions for the yellow knot vector: at a triple knot they pinch to a point, which is what lets the curve turn sharply there. Knots at the bounces cost you knowing when the bounces happen.
